What do these metrics mean?

Understanding your speed test results helps you know whether your connection is right for your daily needs — from casual browsing to 4K streaming and competitive gaming.

↓ Download Speed

How fast your device receives data from the internet. Measured in Mbps. This affects streaming, loading websites, and downloading files. Most households need at least 25 Mbps for comfortable use.

↑ Upload Speed

How fast your device sends data to the internet. Important for video calls, live streaming, sending large files, and cloud backups. Usually lower than download on home connections.

◎ Ping & Jitter

Ping measures the round-trip delay between your device and a server (in ms). Jitter is the variation in that delay. Low ping and jitter are critical for gaming, VoIP, and video conferencing.

What is a good internet speed?

The right internet speed depends entirely on how you use it and how many devices are connected at once. Here are general guidelines:

Basic browsing and email

1–5 Mbps download is enough for light web browsing, reading emails, and social media with a single device. Most modern connections far exceed this minimum.

HD and 4K video streaming

Netflix recommends 5 Mbps for HD and 25 Mbps for Ultra HD (4K) per stream. If multiple people are streaming simultaneously, multiply accordingly. A household of 4 streaming 4K would ideally need 100+ Mbps.

Online gaming

Bandwidth is less critical for gaming — most games only need 3–6 Mbps. What matters more is low ping (under 50ms is great, under 20ms is excellent) and stable jitter. A wired Ethernet connection helps significantly.

Working from home

Video calls on Zoom or Teams typically need 3–5 Mbps upload per participant. If you're uploading large files or using cloud-based tools heavily, 20+ Mbps upload is recommended for a smooth experience.

Tips to improve your connection speed

If your results are lower than expected, these steps often help before calling your ISP.

01

Restart your router

Power-cycling your router clears its memory and re-establishes a fresh connection to your ISP. Do this once a month as routine maintenance.

02

Use a wired connection

An Ethernet cable always outperforms Wi-Fi in both speed and stability. If you're testing Wi-Fi, try running the test again with a cable to see the difference.

03

Move closer to your router

Wi-Fi signal weakens with distance and through walls. Position your router centrally and elevate it off the floor for better coverage throughout your home.

04

Check for interference

Microwaves, baby monitors, and neighboring Wi-Fi networks can interfere on the 2.4 GHz band. Switching to 5 GHz on your router usually helps if you're close to it.

05

Close background apps

Other devices and apps using bandwidth during your test will lower your results. Close downloads, streaming, and cloud sync before running the test for accurate numbers.

06

Contact your ISP

If speeds are consistently well below your plan's advertised rate (especially during off-peak hours), it may be time to contact your provider or consider upgrading your plan or equipment.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is this speed test?

Our test measures real data transfer to and from Cloudflare's global network, which has servers close to most users worldwide. Results reflect actual network performance at the time of the test. For best accuracy, close other apps and run multiple tests at different times of day.

Why is my speed test result lower than my plan?

ISPs advertise "up to" speeds under ideal conditions. Real-world speeds are affected by your router's age and quality, Wi-Fi signal strength, the number of connected devices, network congestion during peak hours, and the physical distance to your ISP's infrastructure.

Does the speed test use my data?

Yes — the test transfers several megabytes of data to measure your speeds accurately. A typical test uses roughly 40–100 MB total. If you have a limited data plan, keep this in mind before running the test.

Should I test on Wi-Fi or Ethernet?

Test on the connection you actually use day-to-day. If you want to know your router's true capacity, use an Ethernet cable. If you're troubleshooting Wi-Fi issues, test both and compare the difference.
